## Operational handbook: applied scenarios and decision guidance

The following illustrative procedures explain how to apply the policy rules above. They are fictional examples, not additional entitlements, deadlines or exceptions. Where a scenario is ambiguous, the controlling rule remains the policy statement above and the responsible owner must clarify it.

### Account protection and MFA

**Rule and interpretation.** Multi-factor authentication is mandatory for company email, VPN and administrative accounts. Passwords must contain at least 14 characters and may not be shared.

**Worked scenario.** A colleague asking to borrow another person’s login is not an exception to the password-sharing prohibition, even for urgent work.

**Employee workflow.** Use individual accounts, complete MFA challenges, keep recovery options secure and report suspicious prompts through the incident channel.

**Owner responsibilities.** IT manages approved account recovery; managers should not ask staff to disclose credentials to speed up a task.

**Scope and limits.** This guide does not set a password-rotation frequency or approve a particular authenticator product.

### Reporting a security incident

**Rule and interpretation.** Suspected phishing, device loss or unauthorized data access must be reported to security@example.test within one hour of discovery. The clock begins when the issue is discovered.

**Worked scenario.** If an employee notices a missing laptop at 14:00, the stated reporting deadline is within one hour of that discovery, not one hour after a later investigation finishes.

**Employee workflow.** Record when the issue was noticed; notify the specified security mailbox promptly; provide device or account identifiers without sending unnecessary sensitive content.

**Owner responsibilities.** The security team assesses containment and follow-up. Managers support prompt reporting rather than waiting for proof that an incident occurred.

**Scope and limits.** Employees are not asked to perform forensic investigation or promise a resolution time.

### Information classification and AI tools

**Rule and interpretation.** Company data has four classifications: Public, Internal, Confidential and Restricted. Restricted information must not be uploaded to public AI services.

**Worked scenario.** A team member drafting a summary must not paste restricted customer records into a public chatbot, even if the intended output is only a short paragraph.

**Employee workflow.** Identify the classification before sharing; use authorized systems; remove unnecessary sensitive content from routine communications; ask information security when classification is uncertain.

**Owner responsibilities.** Information owners classify records and security personnel advise on approved handling.

**Scope and limits.** The rule explicitly prohibits restricted uploads; it does not imply that all other classifications are freely shareable.

### Device encryption and screen protection

**Rule and interpretation.** Company devices must use full-disk encryption and lock automatically after five minutes of inactivity. These are baseline device controls.

**Worked scenario.** A laptop that stays unlocked for an extended unattended period fails the intended protection even if its storage is encrypted.

**Employee workflow.** Confirm the company security settings are active; manually lock the device when stepping away; report settings that appear disabled.

**Owner responsibilities.** IT applies approved configuration and checks compliance. Staff should not bypass controls for convenience.

**Scope and limits.** This guide does not authorize self-installing encryption software or changing corporate configuration.

### Handling ambiguous security requests

**Rule and interpretation.** Urgency does not cancel authentication, data-classification or incident-reporting obligations. Requests for credentials and unusual MFA prompts should be treated cautiously.

**Worked scenario.** A caller claiming to be IT and demanding a password should be directed to the approved support process rather than receiving account secrets.

**Employee workflow.** Pause the sensitive action, verify the requester through a known channel, and notify security if the request resembles phishing or unauthorized access.

**Owner responsibilities.** Security reviews the report and advises on safe next steps; managers preserve a culture of early reporting.

**Scope and limits.** This scenario guidance does not add an unlisted disciplinary penalty or new reporting address.


## Reference workflow and recordkeeping

For any question about this policy, first locate the applicable rule and identify its exact trigger: a calendar-day deadline, a working-day deadline, an approval threshold, a role requirement, or an information-classification restriction. Next, check the relevant record in the approved system and obtain written approval wherever the rule requires it. Keep only the records necessary to show compliance, and share them only with authorized personnel. If the written policy does not address a situation, ask the designated owner rather than extrapolating from an example.

When communicating a decision, distinguish the policy requirement from a manager’s operational choice. Note the policy heading, relevant dates, evidence consulted, approver and any unresolved question. Escalate disputes to the named function where one is specified. The examples in this handbook are educational and do not supersede contracts, applicable law, or approved company policy updates.
